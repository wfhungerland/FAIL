# Let Europe fail better: Building the Single Market's recycling capacity

Wolf-Fabian Hungerland (Federal Ministry for Economic Affairs and Energy, Berlin; Stanford University Bing Overseas Studies Program, Berlin)

*The Single Market frees goods, persons, services and capital to cross borders. It does not ensure that the people, ideas and capital a failed venture releases return quickly to productive use. Building that recycling capacity is a narrow reform with a long payoff.*

A bad business outcome should end a venture, not a career. By 2050, that should be true across the Union: productive assets move quickly from failed ventures to promising ones, viable firms in trouble are reorganised rather than broken up, and failed founders are refinanced within months rather than marked for life.

Call the property that makes this possible institutional recycling capacity: the ability of an economy to return what a failed venture releases to productive use. Every mature market economy needs it, and Europe supplies it only weakly. Since the Draghi report, the competitiveness debate has run to capital: too little investment, fragmented markets, a venture-capital gap. Each is real, yet almost every remedy lowers the cost of success; few address the cost of failure. Brussels has begun to notice: the 2025 Startup and Scaleup Strategy promises to 'reduce the cost of failure' (European Commission 2025), and a 2026 directive harmonises pre-pack sales and other parts of insolvency procedure (European Parliament and Council 2026). Both treat failure as a matter of procedure. Recycling capacity also depends on credit data, on who finances a second attempt and on who decides which firms are saved.

## The money is there; the recycling is not

Europe does not lack savings; it lacks the intermediation that turns them into risk-taking, least of all into second attempts. EU households saved €1,390 billion in 2022, against €840 billion in the United States, yet only 5% of global venture-capital funds are raised in the EU, against 52% in the US (Draghi 2024).

The cost shows at the frontier of innovation. In the last systematic EU–US comparison, using 2007 data, only one in five of the EU's leading innovators was a young firm, against more than half in the United States, and young firms accounted for 7% of leading-firm research spending in the EU, against 35% in the US (Veugelers and Cincera 2010). These are not start-ups but large firms founded after 1975, the survivors of earlier waves of entry. The link to failure rules is direct: across 33 economies, debtor-friendly personal bankruptcy reforms raised patenting, mostly among firms without large patent portfolios, and tilted it towards radical innovation (Cumming et al. 2024). The evidence is firmest that forgiving failure raises entrepreneurship, more indirect that young firms carry the innovation gap, and thinnest for the full chain to growth.

## How Europe treats failure

Three institutions decide how an economy handles failure, and they move together. Bankruptcy law sets the legal cost: more forgiving regimes go with higher self-employment (Armour and Cumming 2008) and higher rates of new-firm entry across countries (Lee et al. 2011). Finance decides whether a failed founder is backed again: in the United States, venture investors offer even previously unsuccessful serial founders better terms than first-timers (Nahata 2019). Culture sets the social penalty: across 23 countries, failed entrepreneurs are less likely to try again where stigma is strongest, and return later where insolvency takes longer to resolve (Simmons, Wiklund and Levie 2014). Recycling capacity is not a fourth institution but what these three produce together.

Europe has already reformed the most visible part. Under a 2019 EU directive, member states must allow insolvent entrepreneurs to obtain a full discharge within three years (European Parliament and Council 2019). But a discharge on paper is not yet a fresh start. A credit file can outlive it, investors can refuse a second attempt, and a viable firm can be liquidated before anyone tries to save it. Estonia, where Skype's alumni went on to found and fund new firms, now has more tech companies valued at $1 billion or more per head than any other European country (Atomico 2024); that is recycled success, and Europe also needs to recycle failure.

## Forgiveness is not subsidy

The obvious objection is that forgiveness rewards bad bets and keeps doomed firms alive. The second half confuses experimentation with life support. Zombie firms tie up workers and capital that more productive firms could use (Adalet McGowan, Andrews and Millot 2018); a faster process that separates viable firms from unviable ones exposes them sooner. The first half has a price: lenders charge for forgiveness. In US states with unlimited homestead exemptions, small firms are more likely to be denied credit, and pay higher rates when they get it (Berkowitz and White 2004). The entry evidence suggests the gain can outweigh that price, provided creditors, not debtors alone, decide which firms are worth saving.

## Three reforms for 2050

None requires a single bankruptcy code, only convergence in function.

First, by 2050 a founder discharged in any member state should regain credit within months, on the same terms across the Union. Stigma and slow resolution are what keep failed entrepreneurs from returning (Simmons, Wiklund and Levie 2014), and a credit file that outlasts the discharge prolongs both. The Court of Justice's 2023 ruling, which bars credit agencies from keeping discharge data longer than the public insolvency register, points the way (Court of Justice 2023).

Second, Europe's long-term savings should finance its own second attempts. Where investors are willing, failed founders get funded again (Nahata 2019). Yet European pension funds put about 0.01% of their assets into European venture capital (Atomico 2024), and insurers and occupational pension funds investing some €10.5 trillion (EIOPA 2025) allocate a similar rounding error. That reflects prudential rules and fragmented markets, not a law of nature; the Savings and Investments Union should make domestic second-round financing ordinary.

Third, reorganisation should be the default for viable firms. A streamlined US procedure that lets small firms restructure under their own managers reaches a confirmed plan about twice as often as small-business cases that stay in ordinary Chapter 11, and months sooner (U.S. Trustee Program 2025). Debtors choose the procedure, so part of the gap is selection, but the speed matters in itself.

This is not a case for Americanising Europe, whose coordinated model supports incremental innovation worth keeping (Dilli, Elert and Herrmann 2018); the aim is to borrow specific functions, not the model.

## Insiders against entrants

Beneath the technical questions lies a familiar choice: whether institutions protect the insiders who already hold a position, or open the way for the entrants who would challenge them. Harsh failure regimes protect insiders: incumbent firms shielded from disruptive rivals, creditors who prefer a quick liquidation to a rescue. The costs fall on those who do not yet form a constituency. Reform becomes feasible once these outsiders are named and organised: the next generation of founders, the workers in firms that could restructure rather than close, and the savers who would earn venture returns at home.

A mature market economy separates the failure of a venture from the failure of a person. A Europe that does so by 2050 would be more competitive and a more confident liberal society: a strong case for the European project itself.

## About the author

Wolf-Fabian Hungerland is an economist at the German Federal Ministry for Economic Affairs and Energy (BMWE) and teaches at Stanford University's Bing Overseas Studies Program in Berlin. The research behind this column applies the varieties-of-capitalism framework to how bankruptcy law, finance and culture shape business failure and renewal in Europe and the United States.
