# Let Europe fail better: Free what business failures release, faster

Wolf-Fabian Hungerland (Federal Ministry for Economic Affairs and Energy, Berlin; Stanford University Bing Overseas Studies Program, Berlin)

*The Single Market guarantees the free movement of goods, persons, services and capital. Its missing freedom is the movement of economic lives across the boundary of failure — the capacity to return people, ideas and capital to productive use once a venture ends. Building it may be the most cost-effective competitiveness reform Europe has not yet attempted.*

A bad business outcome should end a venture, not a career. That is one test of where the European economy should stand a generation from now — the horizon the Europe 2050 project sets. In such an economy, productive assets move quickly from failed ventures to promising ones, viable businesses in trouble are reorganised rather than broken up, and failed founders are refinanced within months rather than marked for life — talent and capital recombined, again and again, into fresh attempts.

What the Single Market has never built is the capacity to put the people, ideas and capital that a failed venture releases back to productive use. Call it institutional recycling capacity — a property every mature market economy needs and Europe supplies only weakly. It is not a fifth legal freedom, and no treaty will grant it; it is a function, and the competitiveness debate has largely missed it. Since the Draghi report the diagnosis has run to capital: too little investment, fragmented markets, a venture-capital gap. Each is real, yet almost every remedy lowers the cost of success while few address the cost of failure.

## The money is there; the dynamism is not

Europe does not lack savings; it lacks the intermediation that turns them into entrepreneurial risk-taking. EU households saved €1,390 billion in 2022, against €840 billion in the United States, yet only 5% of global venture-capital funds are raised in the EU, against 52% in the US (Draghi 2024). European pension funds route a rounding error — on the order of 0.01% of their assets — into European VC funds (Atomico 2024). The capital exists; the institutions that would put it to work do not.

The cost shows at the frontier of innovation. In the last systematic EU–US comparison, using 2007 data, only one in five of the EU's leading innovators was a young firm, against more than half in the United States; young firms accounted for a mere 7% of EU leading-firm research spending, against 35% in the US (Veugelers and Cincera 2010). These are not start-ups but large firms founded after 1975 — the survivors of earlier waves of entry. Europe has fewer of them, and fewer still in the new, research-intensive sectors, so an economy that produces few surviving young firms quietly forgoes the high-variance end of innovation. The evidence is firmest that forgiving failure raises entrepreneurship, more indirect that young firms carry the innovation gap, and thinnest for the full chain to growth.

## How Europe treats failure

Three institutions decide how an economy handles failure, and they move together. Bankruptcy law sets the legal cost: more forgiving regimes go with higher self-employment (Armour and Cumming 2008) and higher rates of new-firm entry across countries (Lee et al. 2011). Finance decides whether a failed founder is backed again: in the United States, venture investors offer even previously unsuccessful serial founders better terms than first-timers (Nahata 2019). Culture sets the social penalty: across 23 countries, failed entrepreneurs are less likely to try again where stigma is strongest, and return later where insolvency takes longer to resolve (Simmons, Wiklund and Levie 2014). Together they form a bundle that either recycles failure or punishes it.

Europe has already moved on the most visible part. Under a 2019 EU directive, transposition due in 2021-2022, member states must allow insolvent entrepreneurs to obtain a full discharge within three years, and the old archetype of long, conditional discharge has largely gone (European Parliament and Council 2019). The binding constraints now lie elsewhere: whether a discharged founder regains credit in months or carries a years-long mark, whether national savings reach risk capital, and whether a viable firm can be reorganised rather than liquidated.

Estonia illustrates the gain — an example of the mechanism, not proof of it. After Skype was built in Tallinn and sold, its alumni recycled capital and expertise into a dense run of new ventures: early Skype staff and founders started Wise and Starship and were among the first backers of Pipedrive and Bolt. A country of fewer than 1.4 million people now counts more start-up unicorns per head than almost any other in Europe (Atomico 2024).

## Forgiveness is not subsidy

The obvious objection — that forgiving failure rewards bad bets and keeps doomed firms alive — does not hold. There is a difference between enabling productive experimentation and propping up unviable 'zombie' firms, whose survival ties up workers and capital that more productive firms could use (Adalet McGowan, Andrews and Millot 2018). The freedom to begin again is not a licence to walk away from debts or shift losses onto others. It is the opposite: the rapid reallocation of assets, the quick reintegration of honest entrepreneurs, the rescue of viable firms under new management, and the removal of stigma that serves no economic purpose — never a subsidy for unproductive activity, which a faster, cleaner process in fact exposes sooner.

## Completing the freedom

Completing it needs no single bankruptcy code, only convergence in function along three lines. First, failure should carry no informational life sentence: a discharged entrepreneur should regain credit in months, not years, and on comparable terms across the Union — the direction in which the Court of Justice's 2023 ruling on the retention of discharge data by credit agencies already points (Court of Justice 2023). Second, Europe's own long-term savings should finance its own risk-taking: insurers and occupational pension funds investing some €10.5 trillion (EIOPA 2025) yet allocating a rounding error to venture capital reflect prudential rules and fragmented markets, not a law of nature, and the Savings and Investments Union should make domestic second-round financing ordinary. Third, reorganisation, not liquidation, should be the default for viable firms: a streamlined US procedure that lets small firms restructure under their own managers reaches a confirmed plan about twice as often, and months sooner, as small-business cases that stay in ordinary Chapter 11 (U.S. Trustee Program 2025).

None of this is a case for Americanising Europe, whose coordinated model has real virtues — incremental innovation, stakeholder coordination, social stability — worth keeping (Dilli, Elert and Herrmann 2018). The aim is narrower: to borrow the specific functions that speed the recycling of entrepreneurial resources, and to modernise the European model rather than trade it away.

## Insiders against entrants

Beneath the technical questions lies a choice that recurs throughout European history: whether institutions protect the insiders who already hold a position, or open the way for the entrants who would challenge them. Harsh failure regimes protect insiders — incumbent firms shielded from disruptive rivals, creditors who prefer a quick liquidation to a rescue. The costs fall on those who do not yet exist as a constituency: the founders, employees and firms of the next attempt. Every widening of the Single Market has met the same tension and advanced by opening access against those comfortable behind the old borders. The right to begin again is the next case — feasible once the coming generation of founders, the workers in firms that could restructure rather than die, and the savers who would gain from venture returns at home are named and joined.

This begins as a narrow reform of insolvency and credit rules, but its effects compound. Easier second attempts mean more entrepreneurs; more entrepreneurs mean more of the young firms that carry radical innovation; more such firms mean a more dynamic economy; and dynamism is the precondition for the autonomous, prosperous Europe the 2050 debate is reaching for. The evidence for the full chain is not conclusive and may never be, but it points one way; what is at stake is the research-intensive young-firm sector, where Europe trails the US roughly fivefold (Veugelers and Cincera 2010); and the reforms are modest and reversible — reason enough to begin.

The deepest point is not merely economic. A union that lets goods, persons, services and capital cross every internal border, yet lets one business failure shut a person out of credit, finance and a second chance, has left something unfinished. A dynamic society does not define people by their worst economic outcome; a mature market economy separates the failure of a venture from the failure of a person. A Europe that lets people, ideas and capital find one another again after failure would not merely be more competitive. It would be a more confident, more mature liberal society — and that may become one of the strongest justifications for the European project itself.

## About the author

Wolf-Fabian Hungerland is an economist at the German Federal Ministry for Economic Affairs and Energy (BMWE) and teaches at Stanford University's Bing Overseas Studies Program in Berlin. The research behind this column applies the varieties-of-capitalism framework to how bankruptcy law, finance and culture shape business failure and renewal in Europe and the United States.
